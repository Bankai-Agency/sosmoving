Office Movers in Tustin Start With an Inventory, Not a Truck

An office relocation in Tustin is priced from what stands in the suite, so the first visit is a count. The coordinator walks both addresses, records desks, chairs, screens, filing cabinets, conference furniture and anything that needs more than two people, and notes where each item has to land.

That count becomes three numbers your side can plan around: crew size, hours and the number of stages. Two movers and a truck suit a small studio suite, while a floor of loaded laterals and a boardroom table usually calls for four.

Departments that must keep working through the move are marked apart from departments that can go dark for a day. So is everything nobody wants to pay to carry twice: broken chairs, dead monitors, archive boxes that belong in storage rather than in the new suite.

The inventory also splits the packing. Desk contents in labelled crates go quickly, while fragile, awkward or very heavy pieces are flagged here, since a single item over 300 lbs is handled by a crew of three at $150 and a piano in a lobby needs four at $200.

Whatever is agreed at the walkthrough goes into the written estimate. Nothing on the day should be a discovery, least of all a locked storeroom or a server closet nobody mentioned.

Labels, Seat Numbers and the IT Pass

Labels are what make the first morning in a new suite quiet. Every box, chair, screen and cable bag carries a seat number and a destination room, and the same numbering goes onto a floor plan taped beside the door at the receiving address.

Workstations are photographed before anything comes apart. Panel hardware is bagged and tied to the station it came from, monitor arms stay with their own desk, and power supplies travel with the machine they feed.

IT equipment moves in a pass of its own. Screens go into padded cartons, docks and peripherals are boxed seat by seat, and rack gear travels upright to a spot where your technicians can get behind it.

We are straight about the boundary: the crew moves and places hardware, your own people or your contractor do the connecting. Scheduling those two around each other is part of the stage plan rather than an afterthought.

The same discipline applies to keys, fobs and access cards. They are listed at the walkthrough and handed over by name, because a locked server closet at 8AM costs more than an hour of labour.

Office Furniture: Taking It Apart and Putting It Back

Office furniture rarely leaves a room in the shape it was assembled in. Desks lose their tops, conference tables lose their legs, benching comes apart along the spine, and every fastener goes into a labelled bag taped to the part it belongs to.

Panel systems follow the same routine. Panels travel flat on carts with tiles and brackets grouped by station, so the rebuild follows the new floor plan instead of guesswork on the day.

Filing cabinets are the usual argument. Laterals travel loaded if the lift and the floor ratings permit; otherwise their contents go into indexed cartons, which costs time but saves a bent cabinet and a hard conversation with the property manager.

Reception counters, lounge seating, boardroom tables and break-room appliances are blanket-wrapped inside the suite. Lift cabs, door frames and floors are covered first, since the condition a center is left in is exactly what its deposit rules are about.

Reassembly belongs to the same booking. Workstations are rebuilt, desks levelled and chairs returned to their numbered seats before the clock stops, so nothing is left for a second visit you did not budget.

What We Need From Your Side Before the Truck Arrives

A commercial job goes wrong in the gaps between two organisations, so we ask for a short list in writing. It names the building contacts at both addresses, the rules they have issued, and the person on your side who can make a decision on the day.

Access sits at the top of that list. We need to know which door the crew uses, where the truck may stand while it is loaded, whether the lift is booked by time slot, and who unlocks the suite at 8AM.

Second comes a seating plan for the new address. Numbered seats on a printed plan turn an unloading crew into a placing crew, and that difference is usually an hour or two of billed time.

Third is a decision about paper and personal items. Desk contents either travel in crates the crew packs, or staff fill their own boxes by an agreed hour the evening before; mixing the two is what leaves a floor half-packed on the morning.

Last comes the disposal list. Furniture and equipment nobody wants should be tagged before the date, because paying a crew to carry a dead printer into a new suite is the easiest waste on any estimate.
