How Santa Monica Curbs, Garages and Dock Rules Shape an Office Move

Parking & permits: holding the curb before the truck arrives

Temporary No Parking signs are issued to residents and businesses to reserve on-street spaces when off-street parking or loading spaces are not large enough. The steps have to be completed no less than 24 hours before the reservation starts, and Traffic Services has to inspect the posted signs inside that window — an inspection with under 24 hours to go will not be enforceable.

Once inspected by SMPD Traffic Services the signs mark a tow-away zone, curb space for a moving truck can be held for a maximum of 72 hours, and non-metered reservations are done in increments of 30 feet. We measure the truck against those increments when the estimate is written, and the signs go up the day before rather than on the morning of the move.

Building rules: loading plans, insurance and lift time

Municipal Code Section 9.28.030 is the source of the Parking and Loading Operations Plan, which is submitted to the Director for review and approval. A plan of that kind gives the dimensions of all passenger and freight loading spaces, the path of travel from the loading space to the locations it serves, and the loading operations themselves: hours, frequency and timing of use, truck sizes and circulation patterns.

It also records driveway gates, parking ticket dispensers, gate arms and payment booths, any valet or attendant operations, and the hours of operation for all users. Where a property keeps one on file, our coordinator asks the management office for those numbers instead of guessing from photographs, and the $50 certificate of insurance goes across in the same email.

Area specifics: campuses, courtyards and underground garages

Santa Monica Business Park at 3420 Ocean Park is a creative office campus of more than 45 acres, 21 properties and 1.18 million square feet, sitting beside the Sunset Park and Ocean Park neighborhoods. Colorado Center at 2401 Colorado Avenue holds six properties on 15 acres and 1.14 million square feet, with about 3,100 parking spaces in a three-level underground garage arranged around a central courtyard.

Addresses of that shape are priced by walking distance rather than by street frontage, because the carry from the nearest legal truck position to the suite door is where the hours go. Our surveyor paces that route, notes lift positions and courtyard gates, and turns the walk into a number before anything is signed.

Logistics & timing: truck routes and oversize loads

The city truck route dataset, drawn from Municipal Code 3.12.680, keeps vehicles over three tons gross weight on designated routes, with a deviation allowed only by the shortest possible route to a pickup or delivery. Primary routes are the Santa Monica Freeway, Olympic Boulevard, Pacific Coast Highway and Lincoln Boulevard from Olympic to the southern city limits.

Secondary routes, meant for loads with a local origin or destination, include Santa Monica Boulevard, Colorado Avenue, Broadway, Pico Boulevard, Ocean Park Boulevard, Cloverfield Boulevard, Stewart Street, Exposition Boulevard and Neilson Way. Dispatch plans the approach along those streets, and anything needing an Oversize Load Permit is flagged early, since the city asks for one to three business days to process one.

Planning an Office Relocation in Santa Monica, Step by Step

Start With an Inventory, Not With a Truck

A business move is sized by what is in the rooms, not by the square footage on the lease. The first pass is a list: workstations, chairs, monitors, printers, cabinets, crates, the contents of the store cupboard, and the pieces nobody claims until the week before.

Walk the suite with a floor plan and mark every item that will travel, every item that will be sold or recycled, and every item that belongs to the landlord and stays. That third column saves more money than any negotiation over an hourly rate, because furniture that stays is furniture nobody pays to carry twice.

Once the list exists, crew size stops being guesswork. Two movers and a truck suit a small studio; three suit a full floor of desks; four suit a move with heavy pieces or a tight deadline. The list also tells you whether one truck will do the job or whether a second, at $200, will save more hours than it costs.

Give department heads a deadline for their own sections of the inventory. People know their own drawers better than any surveyor, and a list signed off by the people who use the room is the list that survives moving day.

Photograph the rooms while the list is being built. A dozen pictures of a server cupboard, a storeroom and a reception wall settle more arguments about what was where than any spreadsheet, and they give the crew a reference when a piece has to go back the way it came.

Labels and a Floor Plan Everyone Can Read

Every box, chair and machine needs two pieces of information: who owns it and where it lands. A simple scheme is a room number on the new plan plus an employee code, printed large enough to read from the tail of a truck.

Print the new floor plan at A3 and tape it beside both doors, with the room numbers matching the labels exactly. Crews do not guess when a plan is on the wall, and the questions that usually stop a move in the corridor get answered before anyone asks them.

Color is worth using where departments share a floor. One colour per team, one number per room, and the unload runs in the order the labels come off the truck instead of in the order the boxes were packed.

Personal items are the exception worth making. Ask staff to take laptops, keys, badges and anything irreplaceable home for the night of their stage, and the crew never has to account for the one bag nobody labeled.

Order packing materials against the inventory rather than by instinct. Archive cartons for paper, monitor cartons for screens, bubble and blankets for anything framed: running out of boxes at four in the afternoon costs more in waiting time than the boxes ever cost to buy.

What the Management Office Will Ask For

Two buildings are involved in a relocation, and both will have requirements. Ask each management office for the same short list: the certificate of insurance wording, the lift and dock booking procedure, the access hours, and the route from the truck position to the suite door.

The certificate is the item that most often holds up a date. Ours is $50, written to the entities the building names, and it is easier to issue two weeks out than two days out. Where a property keeps a Parking and Loading Operations Plan on file, the dimensions of the loading spaces and the path of travel are already written down, and reading them beats measuring twice.

Lift time is the second item. A goods lift shared with a hundred other tenants is a queue, and a booked window turns that queue into a schedule the crew can work against.

Third comes protection. Most offices expect jamb guards, floor runners and lift blankets, and it is quicker to agree that at the walkthrough than to argue about it with a security desk at eight in the morning.

Dock and curb time are booked in the same conversation. A building with its own loading bay will give you a slot; a building without one sends the truck to the curb, and that space has to be reserved a day ahead rather than found on the morning.

Keeping the Business Open While the Move Runs

The point of staging is that nobody sits at an empty desk during working hours. Archives, storerooms and spare furniture travel first, because nobody needs them to answer a phone. Desks and machines follow group by group, so each team loses part of one day rather than everybody losing two.

Stages work best when they are booked as whole blocks inside the 8AM to 6PM day rather than as an overnight sprint. A Saturday stage gives a quiet building, a free lift and a full Sunday in hand if something has to be finished, and staff walk in on Monday to a floor that is already working.

Decide in advance what counts as done for each stage. Desks reconnected, network gear powered, monitors on their arms and boxes off the floor is a clear definition; "mostly moved" is not, and it is the state that eats a second day.

Keep one person from your side reachable for the whole window. Questions about which room a cabinet belongs to are cheap to answer in the moment and expensive to answer twice.

Connectivity is the one dependency a moving crew cannot solve. Order the circuit, the handover and the phone numbers at the new address weeks before the first stage, because a floor of reconnected desks with no line into the building is still a floor nobody can work on.

Office Furniture: Apart, Across Town, Back Together

Most office furniture travels in pieces. Benching systems, panel walls, conference tables and shelving come apart, the hardware is bagged and tagged against the item it belongs to, and the panels travel flat where they take far less truck space than they do standing.

Reassembly is where the new floor plan earns its keep. A crew rebuilding to a plan puts the benching back in the right orientation the first time, and the difference between a plan and a verbal description is usually an hour per room.

Filing cabinets are a judgment call. Lateral files can travel loaded where the building and the equipment allow it, and they are emptied into numbered cartons where they cannot. Either way the drawers are secured before the piece leaves the room.

Heavy single items get their own line in the estimate. Anything over 300 lbs is $150 with a crew of three, and a piano in a reception area or a staff lounge is $200 with a crew of four, because the weight decides the crew rather than the other way round.

Leave a punch list at the end of the last stage. Two or three items usually remain — a monitor arm short of a bolt, a cabinet key in the wrong drawer, a box in the wrong room — and writing them down while the crew is still on site is what turns a move into a finished move.

IT Equipment and Paper Records

Technology moves best when someone has already switched it off properly. Agree a shutdown time with your IT people, photograph the back of anything with more than three cables, and let the people who own the network decide the order in which it comes down.

On the day, screens come off arms, cables are tagged to their machines, and everything padded travels upright in cartons rather than loose on a blanket. Rack equipment is treated as its own stage, with its own window, because it rarely shares a lift trip politely with desks.

Paper records need a chain that can be audited. Cartons are numbered against the inventory, sealed, and checked off at the far end, so a file room can be rebuilt in the order it came apart and nothing is "somewhere in the middle of the truck".

Confidential material is worth moving in a single stage rather than spread across three. One trip, one list, one signature at each end is simpler to explain to a compliance officer than a week of partial loads. Where records have to sit between addresses, storage is the cleaner answer.

Underground garages change the plan again. Colorado Center at 2401 Colorado Avenue puts roughly 3,100 spaces on three levels around a central courtyard, and the Water Garden at 1620 26th Street runs three subterranean levels with over 4,000 spaces feeding directly into each building lobby, under a parking facility manager who is not the office landlord.

Route planning finishes the picture.
