**Short answer:** the clearest moving company red flags are a price given sight unseen, no written estimate, a large deposit or a cash-only demand, a license you can't look up, no real address, blank forms to sign, and a bill that grows once your things are on the truck. One of these is a reason to ask harder questions. Two or three, and it's time to walk away.

California adds a rule of its own. The Bureau of Household Goods and Services (BHGS), the state agency that licenses movers, says it's illegal to give an estimate for a move within the state without a visual inspection of your belongings. So a firm phone quote for your LA apartment isn't just risky. It breaks the rules before the job even starts.

Below are 13 red flags when hiring movers, grouped by when you'll run into them: researching the company, getting the quote, signing and paying, and moving day. Most of them are on the federal regulator's own list, the FMCSA page "Spot the Red Flags."

## Red flags while you're checking out the company

### 1. You can't verify their license

Every household mover in California needs a permit from BHGS, and you can check whether a company is licensed, active, in good standing and insured at [bhgs.dca.ca.gov](https://bhgs.dca.ca.gov/). If your move crosses a state line, the company also needs a USDOT number registered with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration. FMCSA's [mover search](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/protect-your-move/search-mover) shows the registration and the complaint history in one place.

The warning sign isn't only "no license." It's the dodge: "we're getting that soon," a number registered to a different company name, or a claim that your move "doesn't need" one. And it's not a rare problem. In June 2026, BHGS ran unannounced sweeps in the Bay Area and Southern California aimed at movers without a license or proper insurance, and issued 30 citations totaling $110,230 (BHGS press release, July 9, 2026).

A real mover hands over its numbers without being asked twice. For more ways to vet a company, see [5 quick ways to check if a moving company is legitimate](/blog/5-quick-ways-to-check-if-a-moving-company-is-legitimate).

### 2. No real address, no yard, no trucks with their name on them

FMCSA lists several versions of the same problem: a website with no local address and nothing about registration or insurance, offices and a warehouse that are run-down or don't exist, and a phone answered with a generic "Movers" instead of the company's name.

Put the address into a map. A PO box, a virtual-office suite or an apartment building is not where a fleet of trucks spends the night. The same signal can show up late, on moving day: a rental truck instead of a company-owned or marked one is on FMCSA's list too.

### 3. They're a broker, but they talk like a mover

A moving broker is a sales office. According to FMCSA, a broker doesn't have trucks or movers, isn't authorized to transport your goods, and books your move so it can sell the job to an actual moving company. Sometimes it can't sell it, and you're left with no crew on moving day.

Brokers can legally arrange interstate moves if they're registered with FMCSA and say in their ads that they're brokers, with their location and MC number. The red flag is the pretending: you don't find out who is actually doing your move until the truck pulls up. So ask it straight. "Will your own crew and your own trucks do my move?" And get the carrier's name before you pay anything.

### 4. The reviews look manufactured, or the complaints form a pattern

Every review is five stars, all from the last couple of months, all one sentence long. That can be a new name on an old business. Real movers collect reviews over years, some of them critical, and the replies to those tell you more than the stars do.

A few bad reviews are normal. The same complaint over and over (surprise charges, late trucks, damage claims nobody answers) is not. Here's more on [how to read moving company reviews](/blog/moving-company-reviews-how-to-read).

## Red flags in the quote

### 5. A firm price for a home nobody has seen

This is the first item on FMCSA's list: an estimate given over the phone or online, sight unseen. California goes further. BHGS says an estimate for a move within the state requires a visual inspection, and it recommends doing that inspection in person rather than over video or photos.

Don't confuse this with a posted hourly rate. A rate tells you what an hour of a crew and a truck costs; it isn't an estimate of your total. A mover who confidently prices a three-bedroom house from a two-minute call is either guessing or planning to fix the number later. Neither is good news for you.

### 6. A number far below everyone else's

Get at least three quotes and compare them line by line, not just the bottom number. If one is dramatically lower, ask what it leaves out. Stairs? Long carries? Packing materials? The drive to your place?

Every licensed mover pays for the same trucks, fuel, insurance and labor. So how does one of them do the job for half? Usually it doesn't. It bills you the difference later, when your things are already on board. Not sure what normal looks like? Start with [how much movers cost](/blog/how-much-do-movers-cost-cost-of-hiring-a-moving-company-in-2022).

### 7. Nothing in writing, or no "not to exceed" price

FMCSA flags any mover that won't give a written estimate or says it will work out the cost after loading. California's guidance is more specific. BHGS tells consumers that the estimate should include a "not to exceed" price and that you should receive it at least three days before moving day. That price is the most you can be charged unless you ask for extra services, and any change should be written down and signed by both sides before anything goes on the truck.

So a quote that arrives as a single number in a text message, or a promise to "email the paperwork later," is a problem. On a long-distance move, the pickup and delivery dates belong in that paperwork too. More things to look for in the quote itself are in [red flags in moving quotes](/blog/mover-near-me-red-flags-in-moving-quotes).

### 8. They push you to book today

"This price is only good until tonight." Three follow-up calls before lunch. A little guilt because you're still comparing. Pressure works by cutting your research short, and a company that's confident in its price doesn't need it. The flip side is silence: a company that's hard to reach or dodges questions before you've paid won't get easier to reach after. The simplest defense is time: [booking your move well in advance](/blog/how-long-in-advance-to-book-movers) means nobody can rush you.

### 9. The hourly terms are vague

Many local moves are billed by the hour, ours included, and that's allowed. The BHGS flyer for moves within California says moves of less than 100 miles can be charged by the hour, while moves of more than 100 miles must be charged by weight and mileage. But an hourly price only protects you if every piece of it is in writing. Before you book, you should know:

- the hourly rate for your crew size, and whether it changes by day of the week or payment method;

- the minimum number of hours you'll pay for;

- how drive time is charged;

- every fixed fee, such as fuel or a deposit, and whether the deposit counts toward the bill;

- surcharges for specific items (a piano, a safe, anything unusually heavy) and the crew size they require.

If a company gets vague on any of these, that's where the extra charges will come from. It doesn't look like a scam, which is exactly why it's easy to miss.

## Red flags in the paperwork and payment

### 10. A big deposit, or cash and wire transfers only

FMCSA lists a demand for cash or a large deposit before the move as a red flag. A small deposit to hold your date is normal. A big share of the total, paid before anyone has lifted a box, is how fake movers disappear with your money.

One nuance. A cash discount on its own isn't suspicious (we charge less for cash ourselves). Refusing cards, pushing a wire transfer, or wanting cash before the truck is unloaded is a different story. A card payment leaves a record and a way to dispute the charge; cash and wires don't. More on this in [moving company deposit scams](/blog/moving-company-deposit-scam-warning-signs).

### 11. They ask you to sign blank or half-filled forms

Also straight from FMCSA's list. A blank line where the price, the dates or the inventory should be can be filled in later with anything. If a field really doesn't apply, write "N/A" or cross it out, and keep a copy of everything you sign.

On an interstate move, check one more thing. Federal rules require movers to give you the booklet "Your Rights and Responsibilities When You Move" and FMCSA's "Ready to Move" brochure, and FMCSA treats skipping them as a red flag. And at the end of any move, don't sign the delivery papers until everything is off the truck and you've looked it over.

### 12. "Don't worry, everything's covered by our insurance"

FMCSA calls this out by name: a mover claiming all your goods are covered by its insurance. Coverage for your belongings during a move has limits, and they depend on the option written into your contract. Ask the mover which valuation coverage is included, what it pays if something breaks, and how and when to file a claim, and get those answers in writing. If they stay fuzzy, assume the coverage is too. Our guide to [moving company insurance and coverage](/blog/what-to-know-about-moving-company-insurance-and-coverage) explains the options.

## Red flags on moving day

### 13. The price changes once your things are on the truck

"You have more stuff than we estimated" is the last item on FMCSA's list, and it's the classic moving scam: the price goes up after loading, when you have the least leverage. Sometimes it ends with a mover refusing to unload until you pay.

Paperwork is your protection here. In California, the not-to-exceed price is the ceiling unless you asked for more services, and changes are supposed to be written and signed before loading (BHGS). On an interstate move, FMCSA says you don't have to pay more than 100% of a binding estimate or 110% of a non-binding estimate when the mover raises the price after loading; anything beyond that is billed 30 days after delivery.

On an hourly job, also question long unexplained breaks or a sudden "we're waiting for a second truck" right away. The clock is running on your money.

## Already booked, and now you're seeing red flags?

You're not stuck. Roughly in this order:

1. **Check the license today.** BHGS for a move within California, FMCSA for a move across state lines. If it doesn't check out, treat everything else the company told you as unverified.

2. **Ask for the estimate in writing.** For a move within California, that means a not-to-exceed price, ideally three days before the move. No paper, no move.

3. **Keep a trail.** Save texts and emails, pay by card, and photograph your furniture before it's wrapped.

4. **Don't sign new terms under pressure.** If the price jumps on moving day, point to the written estimate instead of signing a new one on the curb.

5. **Report it.** For a move within California, file a complaint with BHGS online or at (916) 999-2041, option 5. For an interstate move, file with FMCSA online or at 1-888-368-7238; a mover "holding my shipment hostage" is one of the complaint examples FMCSA lists. You can also contact the state Attorney General's office.

## FAQ

### What are red flags with moving companies?

The main ones: an estimate given sight unseen, no written estimate, a large deposit or cash-only payment, a license you can't verify, no physical address, a request to sign blank forms, and a price that rises after loading. Most of them are on FMCSA's "Spot the Red Flags" list.

### Can a mover give me an estimate over the phone in California?

Not a real one. BHGS says it's illegal to provide an estimate for a move within California without a visual inspection, and it recommends an in-person inspection over video or photos. A phone call can get you an hourly rate, not an estimate of your total.

### How do I check if a moving company is licensed in California?

Look it up on the BHGS website, bhgs.dca.ca.gov, and confirm the license is active, in good standing and insured. For a move to or from another state, also check the USDOT number with FMCSA's mover search.

### Is it normal for movers to ask for a deposit?

A small deposit to reserve your date is normal. A demand for cash or a large deposit before the move is on FMCSA's red flag list. Ask whether the deposit counts toward your bill and whether you get it back if you cancel, and get both answers in writing.

### Is a moving broker a scam?

Not by definition. Brokers can legally arrange interstate moves if they're registered with FMCSA and say in their ads that they're brokers. The problem is a broker posing as the mover, so you don't know who will show up or who is responsible for your things.

### What should I do if movers raise the price on moving day?

Point to your written estimate and don't sign new terms on the spot. In California, the not-to-exceed price is the ceiling unless you requested more services; on interstate moves, FMCSA says that in this situation you don't have to pay more than 100% of a binding or 110% of a non-binding estimate. If the mover won't unload, file a complaint with BHGS (in-state) or FMCSA (interstate).
